##### Abstract

Macroprudential policy that shores up the domestic financial system may leave firms more exposed to external risk. I document this trade-off using balance sheet data for listed Indian non-financial firms. When macroprudential measures tighten the supply of domestic credit, bank-dependent firms face the sharpest credit rationing and substitute toward cross-border external borrowing in foreign currency. This exposes firms to exchange rate fluctuations. Firms that hold more foreign currency debt suffer larger losses, lower profits and a higher likelihood of distress when the rupee depreciates. I build a two-sector model that incorporates this margin of substitution to analyze its implications. The model captures the trade-off between greater resilience of domestic banks and greater fragility of firms due to currency mismatch on their balance sheets, and is used to quantify the welfare consequences of macroprudential regulations and derive their optimal stringency. Calibrated to India, the welfare-maximizing capital requirement is close to the Reserve Bank of India's current minimum. The results highlight the importance of considering cross-market and currency substitution when designing macroprudential policy in emerging economies.
